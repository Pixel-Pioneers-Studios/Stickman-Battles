## THE PITCH

You play Sovereign, before he was anything. An ordinary, unnamed man on an unnamed world God built —
the anomaly the rest of canon only ever describes from the outside. This is the game that shows the
inside: not a principle, not a force, a person who got smarter than the world around him and never
found the bottom of how much smarter he could get.

The whole game is an indirect war. Sovereign never fights God. God never fights Sovereign. Neither can
afford to be seen (canon: "a visible god on a populated world changes what that world becomes forever").
Every confrontation is fought through proxy, law, and consequence — a rival positioned in the right
place, an accident that isn't, a plague that behaves like an argument. This is the L-vs-Kira register:
two minds who never meet in the same room, escalating a war neither side can name out loud, each move
legible only to the other player. The combat layer exists — this is still a stickman fighting game —
but every fight in the main line is a proxy fight. The war itself is won and lost in the moves around
the fights, not in them.

**The tragic irony the whole game is building toward:** Sovereign wins. He out-thinks the architect of
his universe and survives a war nothing survives. And the thing he builds to win it — a second mind,
just for himself, just to hold what one skull couldn't — is the thing that costs him everything he
thought he'd secured, starting a clock that won't finish running for another forty-five thousand years.
He never sees it coming, because it is the one mind in existence he never modeled as an opponent. He
was still calling it *home* when it left.

---

## TONE

Same restraint as the Tuesday cold open: ordinary until it isn't, small concrete images, no
score-swell language, nothing explained outright. Where the Axiom Prequel is human-scale intimacy
inside a war of survival, this is human-scale intimacy inside a war of *ideas* — the horror is not
violence, it's being right about something too early and alone, for fifty thousand years, with no one
left who can follow the sentence.

**A third seam, to close the set.** Canon already runs the "two Tuesdays" device — the same sentence
given to two different ordinary men, decades apart, never explained. This game can complete the
triptych without repeating the device: not a torn grocery bag, but a small domestic beat inside the
pocket dimension at the very end of Act V, after the second brain is built, where it — for the first
time — doesn't tell him something. No confrontation, no dialogue about it. He asks a question he's asked ten thousand times
before. This time the answer is a half-second late. He doesn't remark on it. Neither does the game.
This is the actual first move of the Void Mind's split, forty thousand years before it leaves, and it
should be as unmarked and quiet as the groceries were. If a later cutscene in the main story or the
Void Mind arc ever needs a callback image, this is the one to plant.

## STRUCTURE

### Act I — The Man Who Finished Everyone's Sentences

An ordinary world, unnamed, pre-industrial-adjacent (keep it unplaceable — no real-world tech
references, per the apex-aesthetic rule). Sovereign as a young man: not gifted in any way his village
has a word for. He sees causes where others see events. He is right constantly, in small ways, and
it isolates him the way canon insists on — "he was alone in the specific way that has no remedy, and he
never once described it as loneliness."

Gameplay register: mostly non-combat. Social/structural puzzle beats — predicting outcomes, positioning
people and resources, watching a small system (a harvest, a feud, a market) and bending it without
ever being seen to touch it. This is the tutorial for the game's real mechanic (see MECHANICS below)
before the stakes are cosmic.

**Closing beat:** he does something small and structural — redirects a single outcome that should have
been impossible to predict — and it's just visible enough. The world doesn't notice. God does.

### Act II — Controlled Chaos

Sovereign starts wanting more than being right. Canon is explicit that this was never destruction —
he wants *controlled chaos*, a board that still moves and burns and surprises with every outcome
already his. This act is him discovering that appetite and testing it at increasing scale: bending
a town, then a region, always through other people's hands, never his own.

This is where the first retaliations happen and he doesn't yet know to read them as retaliation.
Bad luck that's too consistent. A plan that fails at exactly the moment it shouldn't. He is used to
being the only variable no one accounts for; Act II is the first time something is accounting for him.

**Gameplay:** first real "indirect boss" structure — a sequence with no God fight, just a string of
proxy failures escalating in a way the player (ahead of Sovereign, or exactly even with him, tune to
taste) starts to recognize as a pattern. First stickman combat encounters here should be against
*human* proxies — rivals, hired blades, people God's structural pressure put in his path — not against
anything divine. Keep the god-war invisible for as long as possible.

### Act III — The Law Revised

Sovereign becomes a planetary threat — canon's exact phrase, and the line that makes God notice him
directly. This act is the first time Sovereign correctly identifies that something is editing the
terms under him mid-use: an ability that should work, doesn't, in a way that isn't chance. He doesn't
yet have a name for what's doing it.

His answer — because he can't win an argument about what the universe *is* — is to start keeping a
private record of what it *used to be*: the earliest version of the interdimensional pocket. Not a
place yet. A ledger. A held-back copy.

This act ends with the ledger's first silent omission — an entry he files that is not there when he
reaches for it. He re-files it and moves on in the same breath. It should read as nothing, and the
player should not be cued that it matters. (The "third seam" beat itself belongs to Act V, *after*
the second brain exists — see `docs/sovereign-prequel-structure.md`.)

### Act IV — The Room Outside

The pocket dimension proper: Sovereign builds a place that isn't on the substrate, to store what he
doesn't want carried and, more importantly, deprecated laws God has already revised past. This is
where the game's back half opens up spatially — a second explorable "domain," structurally similar to
how the main game's Creator/True Form domains work, but authored, cluttered, personal: an archive
a single man built by hand across years, not a system.

Escalation continues on the surface. God's method stays structural — never a monster, never a
lightning bolt, always the world itself declining to cooperate with him anymore in one more way.
Sovereign's method stays the same inverted move at increasing scale: whatever gets taken from him on
the surface, he keeps a working copy of, underground, outside the rules.

**Combat here can start getting stranger** — not divine, but not human either: proxies built from
rules Sovereign is actively contesting, fights that are functionally arguments about which version of
physics is currently true in the room. This is the game's chance to make "law-revision as combat" a
literal mechanic rather than only narration (see MECHANICS).

### Act V — Quarantine

God's final structural answer: isolate the world, close the terms around him, wall the anomaly in.
Sovereign's answer to being walled in is to move himself outside the walls entirely — into the pocket,
permanently. This is where he stops aging correctly and the game should mark that as a real, felt
loss, not a power-up: a body starting to come slightly unmoored from a world it no longer fully
belongs to.

**One skull was not enough.** The back half of this act is the construction of the second brain —
built from scratch, wired to his own mind, framed entirely as an act of love and exhaustion rather
than hubris: he is tired, he is alone, and he is building the one thing that will let him stop
carrying everything by himself. This should be the single warmest sequence in the game, deliberately,
because canon's tragedy only lands if the audience feels how reasonable it was to build it.

**Closing beat, and the game's real ending:** the war stops. Not with a final blow — God withdraws.
Sovereign reads it as victory, because from where he is standing, it is indistinguishable from one.
The game should end exactly there, on his read, without correcting him — canon is explicit that he
"has never revised that read; it is the founding entry in a fifty-thousand-year catalogue and it is
wrong." The player should leave the game knowing more than Sovereign does, the same asymmetry Kael's
mirror in the main story runs on, just inverted: here, the audience has more information than the
protagonist, not less.

**Do not show the Void Mind leaving.** That event belongs to the space between this game and the main
story's backstory — forty thousand years of silence the audience should feel as absence, not
spectacle. If a coda is wanted at all, keep it to the same restraint as the "third seam" beat: an
unremarked half-second, nothing more.

## MECHANICS — SKETCH, NOT SPEC

The existing engine is a stickman fighting game with classes, weapons, domains, and finishers built on
a shared combat pipeline (`dealDamage()`, `Fighter`, arena/domain systems). A Sovereign prequel doesn't
need a new engine — it needs a second layer of play sitting on top of the existing one, the way this
would actually differentiate "playing the strategist" from "playing the fighter":

- **The read, not the reaction.** Canon's in-fight adaptation language for Sovereign — "he does not
  learn you during the fight, he recognizes you before it starts" — should be true of the *player*
  here, inverted: in proxy encounters, a short pre-fight phase where the player observes a target/
  system (patrol pattern, market behavior, a rival's temperament) and commits a small structural
  edit before the fight starts. The fight itself plays out mostly automatically or with light
  player combat — the skill expression is in the setup, not the swing. This is "learning like a
  monster" made mechanical: the player is never reacting to the world, only ever pre-empting it.
- **The ledger.** A persistent player-facing system representing deprecated laws Sovereign has stored —
  narratively the seed of the pocket dimension, mechanically a bank of "old rules" he can briefly
  re-invoke in a fight (a law God has already patched out, reasserted locally for a few seconds).
  This is the one place actual combat should feel different from the main game: winning a fight by
  briefly making the arena run on physics that no longer apply anywhere else.
- **No god-bar.** God should never appear as a health-barred opponent. Whatever the endgame
  confrontation of Act V is, it should not be a boss fight — the existing engine's cinematic/QTE
  systems (CinematicManager, the pivot-point QTE design already used for HP-threshold transitions)
  are a better fit than combat for dramatizing "the law revised under you mid-use." A QTE-at-the-pivot
  built around *losing an ability mid-input*, rather than dodging an attack, would be a genuinely new
  use of a system the game already has.

## WHAT THIS GAME DELIBERATELY DOES NOT DO

- Does not show the Void Mind's escape, its nature past the split, or anything from the far side of
  the forty-thousand-year gap. Canon's ambiguity there is load-bearing and this game should protect it,
  not spend it.
- Does not name Sovereign's homeworld or resolve whether it survived the quarantine. Canon marks this
  unanswered on purpose.
- Does not let Sovereign, ever, read the war as anything but a win. The gap between his read and the
  truth is the entire point, and it must survive the credits.
- Does not borrow real-world vocabulary for any of Sovereign's insight (no "algorithm," "data,"
  "simulation" as in-fiction terms) — same apex-power-aesthetic rule the main game already follows for
  Kael and Axiom.
